\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A wireless sensor node spends most of its energy on its radio, and in nearly all
deployments its battery cannot be replaced~\cite{akyildiz2002survey}. Lifetime
is decided by how the network organizes its transmissions rather than by any
property of the sensing hardware. Clustering is the standard response: nodes
elect a subset of themselves as cluster heads, members send short-range packets
to their head, and the head fuses them into one packet and pays the single
expensive transmission to the base station. Because that role is expensive it
has to rotate, and the question every clustering protocol answers is which nodes
should carry it this round.

Twenty-five years of answers fall into three generations. The first is
heuristic: LEACH rotates the role by a probabilistic threshold with an epoch
constraint~\cite{heinzelman2000leach}, PEGASIS abandons clusters for a greedy
chain in which each node talks only to its nearest
neighbor~\cite{lindsey2002pegasis}, and TEEN and APTEEN leave the topology alone
and suppress transmissions below a sensed-value
threshold~\cite{manjeshwar2001teen,manjeshwar2002apteen}, with later heuristics
such as HEED refining the election rule without changing its
character~\cite{younis2004heed,abbasi2007clustering}. The second generation
replaces the heuristic with an explicit objective, solving head selection as a
multi-objective problem with NSGA-II~\cite{deb2002nsga2} or encoding it in a
type-2 fuzzy rule base over residual energy, distance and local
density~\cite{mendel2002type2,karnik2001centroid}. The third learns the
selection function instead of specifying it~\cite{alsheikh2014ml}: a
self-organizing map places heads by unsupervised vector
quantization~\cite{kohonen1990som}, a deep Q-network treats rotation as a
sequential decision problem~\cite{mnih2015dqn,sutton2018rl}, and a graph
convolutional network scores candidates from the adjacency
structure~\cite{kipf2017gcn,wu2021gnnsurvey}.

The generations are usually compared by reading their published tables side by
side, and that comparison does not hold. Each protocol was proposed against a
different simulator, field size, node count, packet length and energy
budget~\cite{kurkowski2005incredibles,pawlikowski2002credibility,andel2006credibility},
almost always against LEACH alone and almost always on a lossless channel. More
consequentially, the assumptions that decide the outcome are rarely stated.
Whether a centralized protocol is charged for the uplink that reports node state
can be worth a quarter of the energy budget. Whether fusion is lossless in bits
determines how much a chain protocol gains over a clustered one. Whether
lifetime is reported at first, half or last death can reverse a ranking
outright, and the author chooses which to report. A margin obtained under one
set of these choices is not evidence about the algorithm; it is partly evidence
about the harness.

This paper measures the three generations in one harness. Nine protocols plus a
direct-transmission baseline run over 30 paired trials in each of two channel
configurations, for 600 runs, and over a further 1{,}350 runs in a node-count by
field-area sweep. Run index $i$ seeds the topology, the per-link shadowing and
the sensed-value stream identically for every protocol, so a comparison at a
given index is genuinely paired rather than an average over unrelated worlds.
Fairness is enforced structurally: the engine owns energy accounting through a
single charging function, packet sizing, the aggregation charge, the channel and
its ARQ retries, and the counting of delivered readings. A protocol returns only
a cluster structure and a route shape, and a static audit verifies that no
implementation writes to the energy or liveness arrays. Everything runs on the
same numerical substrate, hand-written NumPy~\cite{harris2020numpy} including
the backpropagation for both learned models, because per-round setup time is a
reported metric. No protocol is tuned to reproduce any published number;
disagreement with the source tables is expected and is not treated as error.
Absolute lifetimes therefore differ from the source papers by construction, and
what is comparable here is the ordering under identical conditions rather than
the magnitudes.

One assumption favors part of the field and we state it up front. Centralized
protocols are not charged for the uplink carrying node state to the sink,
because that state is treated as piggybacked on data traffic the network already
sends. This is standard in the centralized-clustering literature but it is still
a choice, and Section~\ref{sec:validity} measures it: LEACH spends 12.73\% of
its consumed energy on control traffic against 2.33\% to 3.95\% for the
centralized five, so every cross-class comparison carries a subsidy of nine to
ten percentage points of the budget.

Four results anchor what follows and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} restates them
with their numbers. Transmit power is not a background parameter but a
determinant of the ranking, and bracketing it retracts one of our own
comparisons. The mechanism behind that retraction turns out to be the far tail
of the head-to-sink hop rather than its mean, which is where we first looked.
Clustering redistributes lifetime rather than extending it, so no single death
point summarizes it. And the value of clustering is a property of an operating
regime: when the sink is close enough that no link reaches the packet-error
waterfall, the no-clustering baseline outlives LEACH.
